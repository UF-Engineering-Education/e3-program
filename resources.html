Mentor toolkit

What to actually do in the meeting

Mentoring works better with a shape than without one. Every meeting runs the same five moves; only the middle changes. The plans below supply that middle — a focus, the questions to ask, what the fellow brings, and what you both leave with — for every meeting of the year.

The 30 minute shape

  1. Check in

    3 min

    The person before the project. How is the workload, the sleep, the semester? A fellow who is drowning will usually not say so unless you ask first and ask plainly.

  2. Since last time

    5 min

    Walk the actions you agreed last meeting. What got done, what did not, and what happened when they tried. Not a status report — a way into what the classroom is really like.

  3. Today’s focus

    15 min

    One topic, from the plan for this meeting. Resist the drift into five topics at ten percent depth. If something urgent displaces the plan, move the plan — do not drop it.

  4. Decide

    5 min

    Two or three actions, each with a name and a date, written where you will both see them again. “Think about it” is not an action.

  5. Close

    2 min

    Confirm the next meeting, what the fellow brings to it, and anything you owe them. End on what went well — fellows hear the critique long after the praise.

Meeting plans · semester one

Fall 2026 — nine biweekly meetings

One plan per meeting, sequenced against the program calendar so each conversation lands before the deadline it feeds. Open a meeting to see the focus, the questions, and what each of you brings. Treat them as a starting point rather than a script — a real problem in the fellow’s classroom always outranks the plan.

  1. M1. Set up the relationship

    • Mid-August · before classes
    Meeting plan

    Spend the first meeting on how you will work together, not on teaching. Making this explicit now prevents most of the mismatches that otherwise surface in October. Fill in the mentoring compact together, on screen, during this meeting.

    • Ask: What made you want this fellowship — and what do you want to be true in April that is not true today?
    • Ask: What kind of feedback actually helps you? In the moment, in writing, or after you have had time to sit with it?
    • Ask: What stays between us, and what may I share with the program or with your chair?
    • Ask: Where do you want me to push you, and where do you want room to work it out yourself?
    • Ask: What does a bad week look like for you — and how would I know you were having one?
    • Fellow brings: CV, the course they are assigned, and a rough sense of their goals.
    • You both leave with: a completed compact, recurring calendar holds for the semester, and three goals for the year written in the fellow’s own words.

  2. M2. Course planning and week one

    • Late August
    Meeting plan

    The syllabus and the first two weeks, in detail. This meeting also carries a hard deadline: CTE program applications are due Monday, August 24, 2026, so confirm the fellow’s choice is submitted before you finish.

    • Ask: Walk me through the first class. What will students be doing in the first twenty minutes?
    • Ask: What are the three things a student must be able to do by the end of this course — and where does the syllabus say so?
    • Ask: Where does a struggling student find out they are struggling, and how early in the term is that?
    • Ask: Which CTE program did you choose, and why that one over the others?
    • Ask: What is the single thing about this course you are most worried about?
    • Fellow brings: draft syllabus, the first two weeks of class plans, and the CTE application.
    • You both leave with: written syllabus feedback, the CTE application submitted, and a date on the calendar for your classroom observation.

  3. M3. The first weeks, honestly

    • Early September
    Meeting plan

    Three weeks in, the plan has met the room. This is the meeting where a fellow will tell you something is not working — if you make it safe to say so. Say plainly that a class going badly is normal and is not the thing they are being evaluated on.

    • Ask: What surprised you in the first two weeks?
    • Ask: Where did students get stuck that you did not expect them to?
    • Ask: What did you plan and not get to? What did you cut, and how did you decide?
    • Ask: How do you know whether they are learning — as opposed to whether they are satisfied?
    • Ask: What is taking far more time than you budgeted for?
    • Fellow brings: a piece of student work, and one class that did not go the way they intended.
    • You both leave with: one concrete change to try in the next two weeks, and the observation date confirmed.

  4. M4. Pre-observation conference

    • Late September
    Meeting plan

    An observation without a pre-conference collects the wrong evidence. Agree here on what you are looking for, so the fellow owns the focus and your notes answer a question they actually asked. Use part one of the observation protocol. This is also the window in which the fellow attends their own two observation sessions in exemplary engineering courses — ask what they are watching for there, too.

    • Ask: What do you want evidence about? What should I be counting or writing down?
    • Ask: What will students be doing, minute to minute? How long will you talk without interruption?
    • Ask: What would “this went well” look like, described by what students do rather than by how you feel?
    • Ask: Is there anything you would rather I not focus on this time?
    • Ask: Do you want my raw notes, or the organized version?
    • Fellow brings: the lesson plan, the materials students will see, and any context about the group.
    • You both leave with: two or three agreed focus questions, the logistics settled, and the debrief booked within a week of the visit.

  5. M5. Observation debrief and mid-semester feedback

    • Mid-October
    Meeting plan

    Order matters here: the fellow reflects first, then you present what you saw as description, then you interpret it together. Pair it with a mid-semester feedback round from students — taken now, there is still time to act on it, which is the entire point.

    • Ask first: Before I say anything — how did that class go, and what would you change?
    • Then describe: Here is what students were doing at each point in the hour. Does that match your sense of it?
    • Then the focus question: You asked me to watch for one thing in particular. Here is the evidence I collected on it.
    • Ask: What is one thing from that class you would keep exactly as it is?
    • Ask: What would make the change you want to make easier to do?
    • Fellow brings: their own written reflection, completed before they read your notes.
    • You both leave with: two or three specific changes, and a plan for collecting mid-semester feedback from students.

  6. M6. What the evidence says

    • Late October
    Meeting plan

    Turn the semester’s accumulated evidence — student feedback, assessment results, your observation — into candidate ideas for the spring innovation. The strongest projects come from a problem the fellow has personally watched students hit.

    • Ask: What did the mid-semester feedback actually say, and what had you expected it to say?
    • Ask: Which piece of that is worth fixing this week, and which is worth studying properly next semester?
    • Ask: Where in this course do students reliably get stuck, year after year, whoever teaches it?
    • Ask: If you could change one thing about how this course is taught, what would it be?
    • Ask: What from the monthly workshops or your CTE program might apply here?
    • Fellow brings: the mid-semester feedback summarized, and assessment results so far.
    • You both leave with: two or three candidate ideas for the instructional innovation, written as problems rather than as solutions.

  7. M7. Scope the innovation and the question behind it

    • Early November
    Meeting plan

    Narrow one candidate into a question that can be answered inside a single spring course. The most common failure at this stage is scope — a project that would really need three semesters and a research assistant. Work through the project planner.

    • Ask: State the problem as something students do or fail to do — not as a technique you want to try.
    • Ask: What is the question? Could the honest answer turn out to be no?
    • Ask: What would count as evidence that it worked? What would convince a skeptical colleague?
    • Ask: What are you comparing against — a previous semester, another section, the same students before and after?
    • Ask: Is this genuinely feasible in one course while you are teaching two?
    • Fellow brings: a draft problem statement and any literature they have found.
    • You both leave with: one scoped question and an outline of the evidence that will answer it.

  8. M8. The proposal, the data, and the IRB

    • Late November
    Meeting plan

    The practical half of the proposal. The question that quietly decides whether this work can ever be published is settled here, not in April: whether the right approvals and consent are in place before data collection starts in January.

    • Ask: What data will you collect, at what points in the term, and who will have access to it?
    • Ask: Does this need IRB review, and by which route? Have you completed the required training?
    • Ask: If you want to publish this, what has to be done before the first day of class?
    • Ask: How will you keep your grading role separate from your role collecting the data?
    • Ask: What is the plan if the data come back thinner or messier than you hope?
    • Fellow brings: the draft proposal and a data plan.
    • You both leave with: a near-final proposal, the IRB route decided, and training scheduled if it is outstanding.

  9. M9. Close the fall, set up the spring

    • Early December
    Meeting plan

    A deliberate stocktake against the goals set in August, and an honest look at the jump from one course to two. Produce a short written mid-year summary — it becomes the first half of the joint evaluation in April. Use the growth review.

    • Ask: Against the three goals we wrote in August, where are you?
    • Ask: What are you proudest of this semester? What do you not want to repeat?
    • Ask: You go from one course to two in January. What has to change about how you work?
    • Ask: What support will you need in the spring that you did not need in August?
    • Ask: What do you expect your course evaluations to say — and what will you do if they say something else?
    • Fellow brings: the submitted innovation proposal and a written reflection on the semester.
    • You both leave with: a written mid-year summary and spring meeting dates already on the calendar.

Meeting plans · semester two

Spring 2027 — four monthly check-ins

Fewer meetings, higher stakes: the fellow is teaching two courses while implementing and evaluating the innovation. Protect the hour — this is the term in which a busy fellow quietly lets the project slip.

  1. S1. Launch the innovation

    • January 2027
    Meeting plan

    Everything planned in the fall now has to survive contact with a two-course load. Check that the innovation is on the syllabus and in the course schedule, with dates, before week one closes.

    • Ask: Is the innovation actually in the syllabus and the course schedule, with specific dates?
    • Ask: What does the first week of data collection look like, concretely?
    • Ask: With two courses, what are you deliberately not doing this term?
    • Ask: What is the earliest signal that this is not working, and when would you see it?
    • Ask: What did the fall evaluations tell you, and what have you changed because of them?
    • You both leave with: a data-collection calendar and an agreed point at which you would adjust the plan.

  2. S2. Mid-implementation check

    • February 2027
    Meeting plan

    The meeting that saves the project. Ask to see the data rather than to hear about it — a spreadsheet that does not exist yet is far easier to admit to in February than in April.

    • Ask: Show me what you have collected so far.
    • Ask: What has drifted from the plan — and was that the right call?
    • Ask: What are students saying about the change?
    • Ask: What is your fallback if response rates stay low?
    • Ask: When is the Interface Conference abstract due, and what is your working title?
    • You both leave with: the abstract deadline diarized and any mid-course correction agreed.

  3. S3. Make sense of it, and go public

    • March 2027
    Meeting plan

    Analysis and dissemination together. The discipline to practise here is claiming only what the evidence supports — a modest defensible finding is worth more than an overreach, and reviewers can tell the difference.

    • Ask: What do the data say — including the parts that do not support what you hoped?
    • Ask: What is the one claim you can defend, and what exactly is the evidence for it?
    • Ask: Who is the audience beyond this year — your department, ASEE, a journal?
    • Ask: What does the Interface presentation need to accomplish in the time you have?
    • Ask: What would you do differently if you ran this again?
    • You both leave with: a presentation outline and a decision about what happens to this work after April.

  4. S4. Summative evaluation and what comes next

    • April 2027
    Meeting plan

    Two jobs: prepare for the joint evaluation with the fellow’s Department or Unit Chair, and turn nine months into a record they can use. Complete the end-of-year half of the growth review before the joint meeting, not during it.

    • Ask: Walk me through the year. What is different about how you teach now?
    • Ask: What goes in your teaching statement that could not have gone in last August?
    • Ask: What evidence of your teaching effectiveness do you hold, and where does it live?
    • Ask: In the joint evaluation, what do you most want recognized?
    • Ask: What is the next appointment you are aiming for, and what is still missing from your record?
    • You both leave with: the completed growth review, and an agreement about staying in touch past April.

Worksheets

Five forms to fill in together

Each worksheet opens as a page you can type into and then print or save as a PDF — no account, no download, nothing to install. Fill them in on screen during the meeting, or print them blank and write on them.

1. Mentoring compact

Expectations, meeting cadence, how feedback is given, what stays confidential, and the fellow’s three goals for the year. Complete it in your first meeting.

Open worksheet

2. Biweekly meeting record

The one-pager you reuse every meeting: since last time, today’s focus, decisions, and actions with a name and a date against each.

Open worksheet

3. Classroom observation protocol

Pre-observation focus questions, a timed running record for what students are doing during class, and a structured debrief that starts with the fellow’s own read.

Open worksheet

4. Innovation & SoTL planner

Problem, question, what changes, what evidence would answer it, the comparison, IRB route, and a term-by-term timeline. Feeds the fall proposal.

Open worksheet

5. Growth review

Mid-year in December and end-of-year in April, against the goals set in August. The April half is what you bring to the joint evaluation with the chair.

Open worksheet

How to save as a PDF

Open a worksheet, type into it, then use Print and choose Save as PDF as the destination. Only that worksheet prints, formatted for a page, with the site navigation stripped out.

Note: what you type is not stored anywhere — not on this site and not in the browser. Save or print before you close the tab.

Question bank

When you don’t know what to ask

Keep this open in the meeting. Pick two or three questions, not twenty — then stay quiet long enough for a real answer. Silence after a question is doing work; let it run.

  1. When the fellow says “everything’s fine”

    Questions

    Usually it means “nothing is on fire,” not “there is nothing to talk about.” These get past it without making the fellow feel interrogated.

    • What is the part of the week you most dread?
    • Tell me about the last class that did not go the way you wanted.
    • What question have you been embarrassed to ask anyone here?
    • If you had one more day a week, what would you put in it?
    • Who have you not met yet that you should have met by now?
    • What would you be working on right now if nobody were watching?

  2. Teaching practice and the classroom

    Questions
    • What are students doing during the parts of class when you are not talking?
    • Which concept do you re-explain every single year? What have you tried?
    • How does a student who is lost in week three signal that to you?
    • What is your policy actually optimizing for — and is that what you want?
    • Which activity took the most preparation and returned the least?
    • What would happen if you cut a third of the content?
    • How do the students who struggle most differ from the ones you were like as an undergraduate?

  3. Evidence of student learning

    Questions
    • How do you know they learned it, rather than that they liked the class?
    • What is the earliest point in the term you could detect a problem?
    • Show me a question from your last exam. What does a wrong answer tell you about their thinking?
    • What do the students who failed have in common?
    • Which of your assessments would you defend to an outside reviewer, and which would you not?
    • What did the qualitative comments say that the numbers missed?

  4. The innovation and the scholarly project

    Questions
    • What is the problem, stated without naming a solution?
    • Who else has tried this, and what happened to them?
    • What result would make you abandon the idea?
    • What is the smallest version of this that would still teach you something?
    • Where will the data live, and who else can see it?
    • What could you publish from this even if the intervention does not work?
    • What has to be true by the first day of class for this to be possible at all?

  5. Workload, boundaries, and wellbeing

    Questions

    Ask these regularly rather than only when something looks wrong. A fellow on a nine-month appointment has strong reasons not to volunteer that they are struggling.

    • How many hours is this course actually taking you, honestly?
    • What did you say yes to this month that you should have declined?
    • When did you last have a day with no work on it?
    • What is the service request you are afraid to turn down — and would it help if I said no on your behalf?
    • Who is your support here outside of me?
    • What is worrying you that has nothing to do with teaching?

  6. Career and the record

    Questions
    • What job do you want next, and what does that search committee read first?
    • What is in your teaching portfolio right now, and what is missing?
    • Whose career would you like yours to resemble in ten years?
    • Which of this year’s work becomes a line on your CV, and which quietly disappears?
    • Who should know your name by April who does not know it today?
    • What introduction could I make that you would not ask for yourself?

Resources

Where to send a fellow, and where to read up yourself

Vetted starting points rather than an exhaustive list. The UF links are the ones you will use most — they are the services your fellow is already entitled to and frequently does not know about.

At UF

Teaching consultations

The Center for Teaching Excellence will do a confidential consultation or a second classroom observation. Useful when a fellow wants feedback from someone with no role in evaluating them.

teach.ufl.edu

GatorEvals & reading them well

Guides to interpreting quantitative and qualitative evaluation results, plus teaching strategies mapped to specific evaluation questions. Take a fellow through these before their first results land.

teach.ufl.edu

Teaching Beyond the Podium

UF’s own collection of practical teaching guidance — a reasonable first stop when a fellow asks “how do I actually do X in my class?”

teach.ufl.edu

Interface Teaching Conference 2027

Where the culminating scholarly teaching contribution is presented. Check the submission deadline with your fellow in February, not March.

teach.ufl.edu

IRB-02 — behavioral & non-medical

The review board that covers classroom and education research at UF. Settle the review route in November, before any data are collected in the spring.

irb.ufl.edu

IRB training requirements

Required human-subjects training before a submission can be made. It takes longer than fellows expect, so raise it early.

irb.ufl.edu

Teaching Excellence Academy

One of the three CTE program options fellows choose between in August. Applications for 2026–27 were due August 24, 2026.

teach.ufl.edu

Faculty Learning Communities

The other two CTE options — semester-long communities of practice around a shared teaching question.

teach.ufl.edu

Accessible course materials

Practical requirements and how-to for accessible materials — the subject of the September workshop, and worth checking on a fellow’s existing course site.

teach.ufl.edu

On mentoring itself

EER Mentor Network peer guides

Peer-written guides from the Engineering Education Research Mentor Network on the questions that come up repeatedly in engineering education careers and mentoring relationships.

sites.google.com

CIMER mentor & mentee resources

The Center for the Improvement of Mentored Experiences in Research publishes mentoring compact templates, competency frameworks, and evidence-based training materials.

cimer.wcer.wisc.edu

The Science of Effective Mentorship in STEMM

The National Academies’ consensus report on what actually makes mentorship work in science and engineering, with an online guide built from it. The best single thing to read if you are new to this.

nationalacademies.org

National Research Mentoring Network

Mentor training, structured mentoring curricula, and a national network for faculty who want to develop the skill deliberately.

nrmn.net

Teaching practice and scholarly teaching

Vanderbilt teaching guides

A long-running, widely used library of teaching guides — good for handing a fellow something concrete on a specific technique.

vanderbilt.edu

POD Network

The professional body for educational development in higher education — conferences, resources, and a community for faculty who take teaching seriously as scholarship.

podnetwork.org

ISSOTL

The International Society for the Scholarship of Teaching and Learning — the natural home for the kind of project fellows produce, and a route to publishing it.

issotl.com

Advances in Engineering Education

ASEE’s open-access journal for classroom innovations with evidence behind them — a realistic first publication venue for a fellow’s project.

advances.asee.org

Journal of Engineering Education

The field’s flagship research journal. Worth reading together when scoping the project, so the fellow sees what a defensible claim looks like in print.

wiley.com
